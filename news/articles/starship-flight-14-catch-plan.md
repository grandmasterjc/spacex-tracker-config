# SpaceX wants to catch the Ship this month — and says the heat shield problem is solved

**Category:** Starship  
**Published:** August 4, 2026  
**Hero image:** starship-ship-splashdown.jpg  
**Image credit:** SpaceX  

## Lead

Flight 14 could be the most consequential Starship test yet. SpaceX aims to fly it before the end of August and, for the first time, bring the Ship upper stage back to the launch tower to be caught by the chopstick arms — the same trick it has already pulled off three times with the Super Heavy booster. Elon Musk laid out the plan on Tuesday during SpaceX's first quarterly earnings call since June's record-breaking IPO.

## The catch that changes everything

"Assuming we receive regulatory approval to do so, [we] will attempt to catch the Ship with the tower on the next flight, which is tentatively scheduled for the end of this month," Musk said.

Catching the booster was hard. Catching the Ship is harder: the 171-foot upper stage comes home from orbital velocity, through the punishing heat of reentry, and has to arrive at the tower with enough precision for two steel arms to close around it. Get it right and Starship becomes what it was designed to be — fully and rapidly reusable, both halves flying again within days rather than being written off in the ocean.

The catch needs regulatory sign-off before it can be attempted, so the plan is not yet locked in.

## "I'd consider the heat shield problem solved"

SpaceX's confidence comes straight from Flight 13 on July 24. The Ship survived reentry with its heat shield in excellent condition, splashed down softly in the Indian Ocean, and — for the first time in program history — stayed fully intact afterwards, floating on the surface with onboard cameras still rolling. It is reportedly still in one piece today.

"I don't want to jinx it or anything, but I think I'd consider the heat shield problem solved at this point," Musk said.

That claim carries weight. The heat shield has been Starship's most stubborn engineering problem for years, and it is the one thing standing between a successful splashdown and a vehicle you can catch, inspect, and fly again.

## Real satellites, real orbit

Flight 14 also upgrades the payload. Flight 13 carried 20 Starlink V3 satellites but released them on a suborbital trajectory. This time the V3s are meant to go to an operational orbit — the first time Starship will deliver working payloads into space rather than demonstrating that it could.

## The numbers behind the ambition

The earnings call was itself a first. SpaceX went public on June 12 in the largest IPO ever, raising $86 billion at a $1.77 trillion valuation, and now reports quarterly like any other public company.

The debut figures: revenue of $7.8 billion for the March–June quarter, up 92% from $4.1 billion a year earlier, and a net loss of $541 million, roughly half the $1.0 billion lost in the same quarter of 2025. More than half the revenue — about $4.3 billion — came from Starlink. The launch business that made SpaceX famous is no longer the business that pays for it.

## Starmind, and one flight a day

Musk used the call to point further out. SpaceX plans a constellation of orbiting AI data centers called Starmind — a million spacecraft, eventually — with the first operational units launching in 2027. Getting that hardware to orbit would take an extraordinary number of Starship flights, which Musk does not see as the limiting factor.

"We expect the cadence of flights to be increasing rapidly, and probably a year from now, we will be doing at least one flight a day, possibly more," he said.

Ambitious targets are not new from SpaceX, and its stated long-term addressable market — $28.5 trillion a year, nearly $23 trillion of it in enterprise AI — is large enough to invite skepticism. But the near-term test is concrete and only weeks away.

## What to watch

Flight 14 needs regulatory approval, and the date could move — this program's last flight slipped through an abort, a scrub, and a hold before it flew. Turn on launch alerts in Liftoff and you will know the moment the date firms up, and the moment the chopsticks close.
